# Psychological First Aid (PFA) for Crisis Situations

## Overview

This document extracts operational principles from the WHO/War Trauma Foundation Psychological First Aid framework and translates them into concrete procedures for supporting psychological resilience during prolonged crises. Based on:

- WHO/War Trauma Foundation (2011). Psychological First Aid: Guide for Field Workers
- American Psychological Association (2020). Building Your Resilience
- J. Auerbach, E. Miller (2020). COVID-19 Exposes the Cracks in Our Already Fragile Mental Health System

## Core Principles of Psychological First Aid

### Three Core Actions
1. **Look**: Check for safety, recognize signs of distress
2. **Listen**: Actively listen to understand needs
3. **Link**: Connect to practical support and resources

### Guiding Principles
- **Safety first**: Physical and psychological safety
- **Calm**: Promote calm through your own demeanor
- **Connectedness**: Support social connections
- **Self-empowerment**: Encourage self-efficacy and participation
- **Hope**: Foster realistic hope and positive expectations
- **Dignity**: Respect for individuals' rights and worth

## Crisis Psychology Basics

### Common Stress Reactions

#### Normal Stress Reactions
These reactions are **normal** responses to **abnormal** situations:

**Physical reactions:**
- Fatigue, sleep disturbances
- Headaches, muscle tension
- Stomach problems, appetite changes
- Racing heart, sweating
- Startle response to loud noises

**Emotional reactions:**
- Fear, anxiety, worry
- Sadness, grief, depression
- Anger, irritability
- Guilt, shame
- Numbness, detachment
- Mood swings

**Cognitive reactions:**
- Difficulty concentrating
- Confusion, disorientation
- Memory problems
- Intrusive thoughts, memories
- Difficulty making decisions
- Catastrophic thinking

**Behavioral reactions:**
- Withdrawal from others
- Changes in activity level
- Increased substance use
- Restlessness, inability to sit still
- Clinging behaviors (especially in children)
- Return to earlier behaviors (regression)

### Red Flags Requiring Professional Help

**Immediate professional help needed if:**
- Danger to self or others (suicide risk, violence)
- Inability to care for self
- Complete inability to function for extended period
- Psychotic symptoms (hallucinations, delusions)
- Severe dissociation (detachment from reality)

**Seek professional help if symptoms:**
- Persist for more than 4-6 weeks without improvement
- Interfere significantly with daily functioning
- Cause severe distress
- Involve substance abuse as coping mechanism

## Psychological First Aid Actions

### Action 1: Look

#### Assessing Safety
1. **Immediate physical safety**
   - [ ] Is the person in immediate danger?
   - [ ] Are basic needs met (food, water, shelter)?
   - [ ] Is medical attention needed?

2. **Psychological safety**
   - [ ] Is the person feeling safe?
   - [ ] Are they overwhelmed?
   - [ ] Are there signs of severe distress?

#### Recognizing Distress Signs

**Adults:**
- Appearing dazed or disconnected
- Extreme tearfulness or emotionlessness
- Panic attacks or hyperventilation
- Inability to communicate clearly
- Extreme anger or agitation
- Wandering or purposeless movement

**Children:**
- Regression to earlier behaviors (bedwetting, thumb-sucking)
- Clinging to caregivers
- Behavioral outbursts
- Withdrawal from play
- Difficulty separating from caregivers
- Repetitive play related to trauma

**Older adults:**
- Increased confusion
- Withdrawal from usual activities
- Neglecting self-care
- Worsening of existing health conditions
- Increased dependence on others

### Action 2: Listen

#### Active Listening Techniques
1. **Prepare to listen**
   - Find a quiet, private space if possible
   - Ensure physical safety first
   - Position yourself at eye level
   - Maintain appropriate personal space
   - Remain calm yourself

2. **How to listen effectively**
   - Give full attention (put away distractions)
   - Allow silence (don't rush to fill gaps)
   - Use non-verbal encouragement (nod, eye contact)
   - Don't interrupt
   - Focus on their perspective

3. **What to say**
   - "I can see this is very difficult for you"
   - "It's understandable to feel [emotion] in this situation"
   - "You're safe now" (if true)
   - "Take your time, there's no rush"
   - "What do you need right now?"

4. **What NOT to say**
   - "I know exactly how you feel" (you don't)
   - "At least it wasn't worse" (minimizing)
   - "Everything happens for a reason" (invalidating)
   - "You'll get over it" (dismissive)
   - "Stop crying" (suppressive)
   - "You should feel lucky" (guilt-inducing)

#### Responding to Difficult Emotions

**When someone is crying:**
- Stay present
- Offer tissues if available
- Don't tell them to stop
- Let them cry as long as needed
- Offer presence: "I'm here with you"

**When someone is angry:**
- Stay calm (don't take it personally)
- Validate feelings without validating harmful actions
- Don't argue or debate
- Ensure safety if anger becomes aggressive
- "It makes sense that you're angry about this"

**When someone is panicked:**
- Speak slowly and calmly
- Encourage slow breathing
- Focus on immediate present
- Grounding exercises: "Look around, tell me 5 things you see"
- Reassure about safety in the immediate moment

**When someone feels guilty:**
- Normalize guilt as common stress reaction
- Gently challenge unrealistic guilt
- Focus on what they did, not what they didn't do
- "You did the best you could in an impossible situation"

### Action 3: Link

#### Connecting to Practical Support

**Immediate practical help:**
- [ ] Water, food, rest
- [ ] Safe place to stay
- [ ] Information and updates
- [ ] Ability to contact loved ones
- [ ] Access to services and resources

**Information needs:**
- Clear, accurate information
- Avoid rumors and speculation
- Share only what you know to be true
- Help them identify what they don't know
- Help them find reliable information sources

**Social connections:**
- Facilitate contact with family/friends
- Help them reconnect with support networks
- Encourage group support when appropriate
- Respect their preferences about contact

#### Basic Needs and Coping Strategies

**Help them identify what helps them:**
- What has helped you cope in difficult times before?
- Who would you like to have with you right now?
- Do you want to talk about what happened, or would you prefer distraction?
- What would make you feel a little safer right now?

**Coping strategy suggestions:**

**Immediate coping (right now):**
- Deep, slow breathing
- Grounding exercises (5-4-3-2-1 technique)
- Gentle physical movement
- Talking with someone trusted
- Writing down thoughts

**Short-term coping (next hours/days):**
- Maintaining basic routine (meals, sleep)
- Light physical activity
- Structured activities
- Time-limited news checking (1-2 times per day)
- Connecting with supportive people

**Longer-term coping (weeks/months):**
- Establishing new routines
- Processing traumatic experiences when ready
- Resuming meaningful activities
- Planning for future
- Seeking professional help if needed

## Supporting Specific Populations

### Children and Adolescents

#### Age-Appropriate Responses

**Preschoolers (2-5 years):**
- Reactions: Regression, clinging, bedwetting, fear of separation
- Support: Reassurance, maintaining routines, gentle explanations
- Communication: Simple, honest explanations

**School-age (6-12 years):**
- Reactions: Withdrawal, aggression, academic decline, physical complaints
- Support: Opportunities to talk, play, structured activities
- Communication: Honest, age-appropriate information

**Adolescents (13-18 years):**
- Reactions: Risk-taking, withdrawal, acting out, substance use
- Support: Respect for autonomy, opportunities to help, peer connection
- Communication: Honest dialogue, involve in planning

#### Parent/Guardian Guidance
1. **Model calm behavior**: Children look to adults for cues
2. **Maintain routines**: Provides sense of security
3. **Provide honest information**: Age-appropriate, reassuring
4. **Listen to concerns**: Take their worries seriously
5. **Encourage expression**: Through play, drawing, talking
6. **Monitor exposure**: Limit news and frightening images

### Older Adults

#### Special Considerations
- May have existing mental health conditions
- May be more isolated
- May have physical health limitations
- May have experienced previous crises
- May resist asking for help

#### Support Strategies
- Acknowledge their experience and wisdom
- Provide clear, repeated information
- Ensure access to medications and healthcare
- Maintain connection to support networks
- Respect their autonomy and dignity
- Be patient with cognitive changes

### People with Disabilities

#### Accessibility Considerations
- **Physical**: Ensure physical access to support
- **Sensory**: Provide information in appropriate format
- **Cognitive**: Simplify information, repeat as needed
- **Communication**: Use preferred communication method

#### Support Strategies
- Understand their specific support needs
- Ensure equipment and assistance needs are met
- Involve support people/caregivers as appropriate
- Respect independence and dignity
- Provide consistent support personnel

## Community Resilience

### Building Community Support

#### Mutual Aid Principles
1. **Neighbor helping neighbor**: Check on those who may need help
2. **Skill sharing**: Use community skills and resources
3. **Shared responsibility**: Everyone contributes what they can
4. **Inclusion**: Include vulnerable community members
5. **Clear coordination**: Organize rather than react

#### Community Response Strategies
- **Communication networks**: Phone trees, group chats, neighborhood groups
- **Resource inventories**: Who has what skills, tools, resources
- **Vulnerable population registries**: Those who need extra support
- **Regular check-ins**: Structured check-in schedules
- **Shared activities**: Communal meals, support meetings

### Dealing with Grief and Loss

#### Types of Loss in Crises
- Loss of loved ones
- Loss of home/possessions
- Loss of routine/normalcy
- Loss of sense of safety
- Loss of community connection
- Loss of future plans

#### Supporting Grieving People
- **Acknowledge the loss**: "I'm sorry for your loss"
- **Listen without judgment**: Let them express their feelings
- **Avoid clichés**: No "at least", "everything happens for a reason"
- **Offer practical help**: Specific offers better than "let me know"
- **Be present**: Sometimes just being there is enough
- **Recognize differences**: Everyone grieves differently

#### Complicated Grief
**Professional help needed if:**
- Grief remains intense and raw for extended period
- Inability to function in daily life
- Extreme avoidance or preoccupation with loss
- Self-destructive behaviors
- No improvement over time

## Self-Care for Helpers

### Preventing Compassion Fatigue

#### Recognizing Compassion Fatigue
- Emotional exhaustion
- Reduced sense of personal accomplishment
- Withdrawal from others
- Chronic physical symptoms
- Irritability and anger
- Intrusive images/memories

#### Self-Care Strategies
1. **Set limits**: Recognize your capacity, set boundaries
2. **Take breaks**: Regular rest periods
3. **Maintain self-care**: Basic needs (sleep, food, exercise)
4. **Seek support**: Peer support, professional help
5. **Process experiences**: Talk with others, journal
6. **Maintain perspective**: You can't fix everything

### Helper Boundaries

#### Appropriate Boundaries
- **Time limits**: You can't be available 24/7
- **Role clarity**: You're support, not a savior
- **Scope**: You can help with coping, not fix the crisis
- **Personal safety**: Physical and emotional safety

#### Signs of Boundary Issues
- Feeling overly responsible for others
- Difficulty ending interactions
- Taking work home emotionally
- Neglecting your own needs
- Rescuing behaviors

## Implementation Checklist

### Immediate Response (First 24-48 Hours)
- [ ] Ensure physical safety
- [ ] Provide basic needs (food, water, shelter)
- [ ] Make human connection ("I'm here")
- [ ] Listen without judgment
- [ ] Validate feelings and reactions
- [ ] Connect with practical support
- [ ] Facilitate contact with loved ones

### Short-Term Support (First Week)
- [ ] Check in regularly
- [ ] Help establish routines
- [ ] Provide accurate information
- [ ] Encourage appropriate coping
- [ ] Identify additional support needs
- [ ] Connect with community resources
- [ ] Monitor for red flags

### Longer-Term Recovery (Weeks to Months)
- [ ] Support gradual return to normal
- [ ] Encourage processing when ready
- [ ] Help plan for future
- [ ] Maintain supportive connection
- [ ] Monitor for ongoing difficulties
- [ ] Refer to professional help if needed
- [ ] Foster hope and realistic expectations

## Key Reminders

### DO
- Do prioritize physical and psychological safety
- Do remain calm yourself
- Do listen actively without judgment
- Do validate feelings and reactions
- Do connect to practical support
- Do respect cultural differences
- Do empower people to help themselves
- Do recognize your own limits

### DO NOT
- Do not force people to talk
- Do not give false reassurance
- Do not make assumptions about needs
- Do not take away choices and control
- Do not separate children from families
- Do not judge or criticize reactions
- Do not use "should" statements
- Do not promise what you can't deliver

## Communication Guidelines

### Ground Rules for Crisis Communication
1. **Be honest**: Don't lie or make promises you can't keep
2. **Be clear**: Simple, direct language
3. **Be consistent**: Same information to everyone
4. **Be compassionate**: Acknowledge suffering and difficulty
5. **Be hopeful**: Realistic hope, not false optimism
6. **Be culturally sensitive**: Respect cultural differences

### Sample Scripts

**Opening contact:**
"I'm [name]. I'm here to help. How are you doing right now?"

**Validating feelings:**
"It's completely understandable to feel [emotion] given what you've been through."

**Offering support:**
"I can't fix what happened, but I can sit with you while you're dealing with it."

**Setting boundaries:**
"I need to take a break now, but I'll check back on you in [time period]."

**Ending contact:**
"You've been through something very difficult. It makes sense that you're feeling this way. I'm going to check on [practical need] and come back in a few minutes."

---

Psychological First Aid is about **human connection** in crisis situations. These guidelines provide structure, but the most important element is genuine human concern and support. Remember: **You don't need to be a mental health professional to provide psychological support.** Most people just need someone to listen, care, and help them access basic needs.
